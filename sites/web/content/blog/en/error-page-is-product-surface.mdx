3am, prod went down for four minutes. The gateway returned nginx's default page: "502 Bad Gateway" in white-on-black, monolithic heading, no trace of a brand. Someone screenshotted it and posted with the caption "KB Labs production, very polished".

Fixed by morning. Too late — the screenshot already had 1,200 views.

This episode is about how teams treat 5xx pages as a **technical detail to be styled someday**, and why that's a mistake.

## The shallow read

"Yeah, we know we should make a nice 500 page. When there's time. Right now the priority is features."

Wrong categorization. The 500 page isn't cosmetics. It's part of the **product surface**, and it's shown precisely when the product **most needs trust**.

## The deeper read

Think about who sees a 5xx page. It's:

- A user who just clicked something and didn't get an answer.
- A customer with a report-via-your-API deadline in an hour.
- A journalist who landed on your site for the first time from a tweet.
- A senior engineer who is right now deciding whether to recommend you to their team.

Every one of them, in that moment, has the **maximum option to leave**. If you return a monolithic "Internal Server Error 500", you're telling them: "we didn't notice this mattered." And they leave.

The error page is **the last thing they see** before closing the tab or writing something about you in public. Treating it as a debug screen is ignoring the audience at the moment of peak sensitivity.

## What should be on the page

You don't need a monument. You need it to be visible that the team didn't forget about this moment.

**Brand.** Logo, colors, typeface. Recognition is the signal "we're here, we see this."

**Voice.** Not `Internal Server Error 500`. Something like:

> Something broke on our side. We already know and we're fixing it. Please try again in a minute.

Direct, no jargon. If you know the upstream is down, say so — don't hide behind vague "an error has occurred".

**Request ID.** A short identifier the user can copy and send to support. It turns a scary moment into a useful one — the user becomes a participant in the fix instead of a victim of it.

**What shouldn't be there.** No stack traces, no framework names, no `X-Powered-By`, `X-Runtime`, `X-Debug-Token` headers. That's both a security risk (attack surface map) and a signal "we don't control what we return".

**API clients.** If the request has `Accept: application/json`, return JSON, not HTML. HTML in a JSON client's parser produces a second bug on top of the first one. Clean JSON with the same fields (error, message, request_id) — about five lines of nginx config.

## Once, then forget

The good news: this is one-time work. One HTML file, one JSON fallback, ~30 lines of nginx config with `proxy_intercept_errors on` and `error_page 500 502 503 504 = @handler`. After that your entire infrastructure automatically returns a branded page on any upstream error — gateway, web, docs, future services.

Invest once and **never again** accidentally show a user nginx's black-and-white screen.

## The principle

> An error page is a **product surface**, not part of the debug infrastructure. It's seen by the people deciding whether to stay or leave, at the moment of peak sensitivity. Making it good costs a day. Not making it costs reputation.

This is the kind of work that's easy to defer — it doesn't ship features, it doesn't move dashboard metrics. But not doing it means that every time you have a prod incident, you also turn the trust dial down on yourself, by hand.

Don't give users extra reasons to write you off.
